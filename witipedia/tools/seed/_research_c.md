## 1. Jacqueline Kennedy Onassis

**Odd detail that can carry an article.** The pink suit was given to the National Archives with a note on her mother’s stationery: “Jackie’s suit and bag- worn November 22nd, 1963.” The same box holds the jacket, the skirt, a blue blouse, stockings wrapped in a white towel, blue shoes, and a blue purse. The pink pillbox hat is not in the gift.

**Shaky.** “Never cleaned” and the precise storage temperature are CNN’s 2013 reporting, not language in the deed. The year 2103 is CNN’s reading of a 100-year bar; the scanned deed’s signature date did not OCR cleanly, so the deed itself is cited for “one hundred (100) years,” not for the calendar year. The famous line about wanting people to “see what they have done” is Philip Shenon, quoted by CNN, not a document in her hand.

1. Caroline Kennedy’s deed gives the United States “the clothing and related historical materials” worn by Jacqueline B. Kennedy on 22 November 1963, “for deposit in the National Archives of the United States.” Access is restricted “for one hundred (100) years from the date of the execution of this deed of gift.” During that period, “None of the Materials shall be placed on public display for any reason whatsoever,” and there is “no access to the Materials for research or scholarly purposes,” except for official investigations of President Kennedy’s death. The materials “shall be maintained … in the National Archives Building in College Park, Maryland.”
   - National Archives and Records Administration, deed of gift (FOIA release). Acceptance date line is present but did not transcribe cleanly.
   - https://www.archives.gov/files/foia/pdf/kennedy-dress.pdf

2. Appendix A lists: “Pink Chanel Suit- Jacket”; “Pink Chanel Suit- Skirt”; “Blue Blouse”; “Pair of Stockings, wrapped in a white towel”; “Pair of Blue Shoes”; “Blue Purse”; part of the cardboard box addressed to “Mrs. John F. Kennedy, The White House”; and stationery of “Mrs. Hugh D. Auchincloss” with the handwritten note “Jackie's suit and bag- worn November 22nd, 1963.”
   - Same deed, Appendix A.
   - https://www.archives.gov/files/foia/pdf/kennedy-dress.pdf

3. “The suit today is in the National Archives building in Maryland.” “In 2003, nine years after her mother’s death, Caroline Kennedy gave the suit as a gift to the people of the United States with the understanding that it would not be put on display until 2103.” CNN also reports the hat is missing: “The hat apparently goes to the Secret Service initially and the Secret Service turns it over to Mrs. Kennedy’s private secretary, and then it disappears.”
   - CNN, Randi Kaye, published 21 November 2013, updated the same day.
   - https://www.cnn.com/2013/11/21/us/jacqueline-kennedy-pink-suit

4. The White House Historical Association “was established in 1961 for the publication of the guidebook.” Mrs. Kennedy told *Life*: “Everything in the White House must have a reason for being there. It would be sacrilege merely to redecorate it—a word I hate. It must be restored, and that has nothing to do with decoration. That is a question of scholarship.” Within two weeks of hiring decorator Dorothy “Sister” Parish, “the $50,000 budget had been spent on refurbishing the private living quarters.” Her CBS tour, aired 14 February 1962, “drew more than 80 million viewers.”
   - John F. Kennedy Presidential Library, “The White House Restoration.” Page opened 9 October 2026; the library does not print a separate article date.
   - https://www.jfklibrary.org/learn/about-jfk/jfk-in-history/the-white-house-restoration

5. On Friday 29 November 1963, “from eight-thirty until nearly midnight,” at Hyannis Port, she told Theodore H. White she mistrusted history as “Something that bitter old men write,” and asked that the Kennedy years be remembered through Alan Jay Lerner’s lyric “Don’t let it be forgot, that once there was a spot / For one brief shining moment that was known as Camelot.” Arthur Schlesinger writes that “no one while J.F.K. was alive thought of his Washington as Camelot,” and that she later “told John Kenneth Galbraith that she had overdone Camelot.”
   - Arthur M. Schlesinger, “Camelot Revisited,” *The New Yorker*, 29 May 1995 (issue date 5 June 1995).
   - https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1995/06/05/camelot-revisited

6. After a New York court overturned Grand Central’s landmark status in February 1975, she called Municipal Art Society director Kent Barwick. The Society formed the Committee to Save Grand Central Station. At the Oyster Bar she said, “If we don’t care about our past, we cannot hope for the future.” Supporters then took a train they called the “Landmark Express” to Washington for the Supreme Court hearing. The Landmarks Law “was upheld, saving Grand Central Terminal.”
   - Municipal Art Society of New York, Jacqueline Kennedy Onassis Medal page. Page opened 9 October 2026.
   - https://www.mas.org/awards/jacqueline-kennedy-onassis-medal/

7. Colleagues who knew her at Doubleday said they met her “after she became a senior editor at Doubleday in the early 1980s.” At a dinner given by Mayor Ed Koch she introduced herself: “I'm Jacqueline Onassis and I'm a senior editor at Doubleday. I love to do books that preserve the heritage of story-telling.” The same transcript says she worked on about ten books at Viking before moving, and that she brought Egyptian Nobel laureate Naguib Mahfouz to Doubleday.
   - John F. Kennedy Presidential Library forum transcript, “The Literary Life of Jacqueline Kennedy Onassis.” Speakers include Greg Lawrence. Page opened 9 October 2026; the library page does not print the forum’s calendar date in the text retrieved.
   - https://www.jfklibrary.org/events-and-awards/kennedy-library-forums/browse-all-forums/transcripts/the-literary-life-of-jacqueline-kennedy-onassis

8. A separate odd workplace detail from the same forum: Harriett Rubin said that at editorial meetings Jackie proposed “an American Pléiade,” uniform hardcovers of classic American authors, “ten years at least, before the Library of America got started.” Rubin also recalled Jackie, seeing her in a red Chanel suit, saying, “Harriet, you look like the Daughter of the Regiment.”
   - Same JFK Library forum transcript.
   - https://www.jfklibrary.org/events-and-awards/kennedy-library-forums/browse-all-forums/transcripts/the-literary-life-of-jacqueline-kennedy-onassis

The 12 February 1978 *New York Times* story “Mrs. Onassis Joining Doubleday as Editor” was found but the page timed out, so the 1978 start date is not treated as verified here.

## 2. Ronald Reagan

**Odd detail.** Blueberry Jelly Belly beans were developed in 1980 so the red (Very Cherry) and white (Coconut) beans could make a flag. Three and a half tons of red, white, and blue beans were shipped to Washington for the 1981 inaugural. His favorite flavor, the Reagan Library says, was licorice.

**Shaky.** The “77 saves” figure is the Reagan Library’s own biographical sketch, not a surviving lifeguard log opened for this sheet. The notch-in-a-log detail appears on the Foundation’s life-and-times page in search results; that page was not the one opened. The Berlin draft story is Peter Robinson’s 2007 first-person account in the National Archives magazine *Prologue*, not the speech file itself.

1. On the morning of 11 August 1984, at his ranch in California, during a sound check for the Saturday radio address, Reagan said: “My fellow Americans, I am pleased to tell you I have signed legislation to outlaw Russia forever. We begin bombing in five minutes.” The State Department’s *Foreign Relations of the United States* note says at least two networks were already recording. His diary, quoted there: “Doing a voice level with no thought that anyone other than the few people in the room would hear I ad libbed jokingly something about the Soviets. The networks had a line open & recorded it and of course made it public—hence an international incident.”
   - Office of the Historian, U.S. Department of State, *FRUS, 1981–1988*, Volume IV, Document 263, editorial note.
   - https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1981-88v04/d263

2. United Press International, 13 August 1984, confirmed the same wording and reported that the real address began, “I am pleased to tell you that today I signed legislation that will allow student religious groups to begin enjoying a right they've too long been denied.” UPI reported that press secretary Larry Speakes “asked the networks to kill the story,” that Speakes denied it (“I didn't do anything”), and that a 1982 network agreement treated off-the-cuff remarks just before the radio speech as off the record. TASS’s 15 August statement, quoted in the *FRUS* note, called the episode a manifestation of “calls for a ‘crusade.’”
   - Ira R. Allen, UPI, 13 August 1984; *FRUS* Document 263 for the TASS quotation.
   - https://www.upi.com/Archives/1984/08/13/President-Reagan-cracked-a-joke-about-bombing-the-Soviet/2768461217600/
   - https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1981-88v04/d263

3. “Three and a half tons of red, white, and blue Jelly Belly jelly beans were shipped to Washington, DC for the 1981 Inaugural festivities. Blueberry … was developed in 1980.” “President Reagan's favorite Jelly Belly flavor was licorice.” He had started the habit in the 1966 California campaign “as part of his successful attempt to give up pipe smoking.”
   - Ronald Reagan Presidential Library and Museum, “Jelly Belly Jelly Beans and Ronald Reagan.” Page opened 9 October 2026.
   - https://www.reaganlibrary.gov/reagans/ronald-reagan/jelly-bellyr-jelly-beans-and-ronald-reagan

4. “Summer Young Ronald Reagan worked his first job at Lowell Park in Dixon as a lifeguard on the Rock River. In the next seven summers he was credited with saving seventy-seven lives.” The same timeline gives his birth as 6 February 1911 in Tampico, Illinois.
   - Ronald Reagan Presidential Library, “Reagan's Pre-Presidential Biographical Sketch & Timeline, 1911-1980.”
   - https://www.reaganlibrary.gov/reagans/ronald-reagan/reagans-pre-presidential-biographical-sketch-timeline-1911-1980

5. Robinson, assigned the Berlin speech in April 1987, writes that an early draft said “Herr Gorbachev, bring down this wall,” then “Herr Gorbachev, take down this wall.” Speechwriting director Tony Dolan put the English line back in: “when you're writing for the President of the United States, give him his big line in English.” State and the NSC “submitted their own alternate drafts—my journal records that there were no fewer than seven—including one written by the diplomat in Berlin. In each, the call to tear down the wall was missing.” One substitute line was “One day, this ugly wall will disappear.”
   - Peter Robinson, “Tear Down This Wall,” *Prologue*, National Archives, Summer 2007, Vol. 39, No. 2. Page last reviewed 9 February 2023.
   - https://www.archives.gov/publications/prologue/2007/summer/berlin.html

6. On 18 May 1987, in the Oval Office, Reagan told the speechwriters, “That wall has to come down. That's what I'd like to say to them.” On 5 June, after George Shultz objected again, Kenneth Duberstein recalls Reagan saying, “Let's leave it in.” In the car to the Wall on 12 June 1987: “The boys at State are going to kill me, but it's the right thing to do.” The delivered line, quoted by Robinson: “Mr. Gorbachev, open this gate! Mr. Gorbachev, tear down this wall!”
   - Same *Prologue* article.
   - https://www.archives.gov/publications/prologue/2007/summer/berlin.html

## 3. Theodore Roosevelt

**Odd detail.** The object that survived is not a legend in a caption. The Theodore Roosevelt Birthplace holds the typescript he carried in Milwaukee on 14 October 1912. The catalog says it “was folded in his jacket pocket at the time of the attempted assassination. Bullet holes indicate how the speech helped to break the force of the bullet. The speech is now bound and presented as a book.”

**Shaky.** That catalog record lists a page count of 12 and a creation date of 12 October 1912. A long narrative on the Theodore Roosevelt Presidential Library site says a “50-page” manuscript and a speech of “eighty to ninety minutes.” The stenographic text he actually delivered, at the American Presidency Project, is explicitly “differing considerably from the prepared manuscript.” Treat “50 pages” and “ninety minutes” as the Library narrative’s figures, not as the count on the surviving object. The *New York Times* of 15 October 1912 was found in search (“went on, for fifty minutes”) but the page timed out, so that contemporary duration is not used as a verified figure here.

1. Opening of the Milwaukee speech, 14 October 1912: “Friends, I shall ask you to be as quiet as possible. I don’t know whether you fully understand that I have just been shot; but it takes more than that to kill a Bull Moose. But fortunately I had my manuscript, so you see I was going to make a long speech, and there is a bullet—there is where the bullet went through—and it probably saved me from it going into my heart. The bullet is in me now, so that I cannot make a very long speech, but I will try my best.”
   - American Presidency Project, University of California, Santa Barbara, text from Theodore Roosevelt, *Newer Roosevelt Messages*, ed. William Griffith (Current Literature Publishing Co., 1919), with a gap filled from *Progressive Principles* (1913). The project’s note says this is “a stenographic report, differing considerably from the prepared manuscript.”
   - https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/the-progressive-cause-greater-than-any-individual-remarks-milwaukee-wi

2. Later in the same text: “Don’t you make any mistake. Don’t you pity me. I am all right. I am all right and you cannot escape listening to the speech either.” And: “No man has had a happier life than I have led; a happier life in every way.”
   - Same American Presidency Project document.
   - https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/the-progressive-cause-greater-than-any-individual-remarks-milwaukee-wi

3. Birthplace catalog: “Typescript of a speech carried by Theodore Roosevelt in Milwaukee on October 14, 1912.” “Bullet holes indicate how the speech helped to break the force of the bullet.” Page count on that record: 12. Repository: Theodore Roosevelt Birthplace National Historic Site.
   - Theodore Roosevelt Center digital library, record o284906. Creation date on the record: 12 October 1912.
   - https://www.theodorerooseveltcenter.org/digital-library/o284906/

4. The Presidential Library’s narrative says he folded “his carefully prepared 50-page typed speech manuscript” over “his steel-reinforced eyeglass case,” was shot outside the Gilpatrick Hotel, and told the crowd, “it takes more than that to kill a Bull Moose.” It says he spoke “for the next eighty to ninety minutes,” and that doctors left the bullet in. Use this beside item 3, not instead of it.
   - Theodore Roosevelt Presidential Library, “The Assassination Attempt: Milwaukee, October 14, 1912.” Page opened 9 October 2026.
   - https://www.trlibrary.com/tr/post-presidency/assassination-attempt

5. “With the assassination of President McKinley, Theodore Roosevelt, age 42, became the youngest president in the nation’s history.” Born “October 27, 1858 in New York City.” He “won the Nobel Peace Prize for mediating the Russo-Japanese War, the first American president to receive this honor.” He liked to quote, “Speak softly and carry a big stick.”
   - White House Historical Association, “Theodore Roosevelt.”
   - https://www.whitehousehistory.org/bios/theodore-roosevelt

6. Clifford K. Berryman’s cartoon “Drawing the line in Mississippi,” dated 16 November 1902, “depicts President Roosevelt refusing to shoot a bear being held tied up.” The Theodore Roosevelt Center’s record says the item “was drawn after Roosevelt’s famous hunting trip to Mississippi and inspired the creation of the teddy bear.” Collection: Library of Congress Manuscript Division. Creator: Clifford Kennedy Berryman, 1869–1949.
   - Theodore Roosevelt Center, record of the Berryman cartoon.
   - https://www.theodorerooseveltcenter.org/digital-library/o274688/

The Library of Congress Prints and Photographs record for the same cartoon (https://www.loc.gov/pictures/item/2008678324/) timed out twice. The Center record above is the page actually read. It says the cartoon inspired the toy; it does not name Morris Michtom or give a sales figure.

## 4. Artificial intelligence

**Odd detail.** John McCarthy, hosting the text on his own Stanford site, wrote: “The proposal for the Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence was, so far as I know, the first use of phrase Artificial Intelligence.” The proposal itself is dated 31 August 1955 and asks for a summer in 1956. The name is older than the conference.

**Shaky.** Nothing here treats the two-month, ten-person plan as something that happened as written. The opened document is the proposal, not a report that ten people finished the work in two months.

1. “We propose that a 2 month, 10 man study of artificial intelligence be carried out during the summer of 1956 at Dartmouth College in Hanover, New Hampshire. The study is to proceed on the basis of the conjecture that every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it. An attempt will be made to find how to make machines use language, form abstractions and concepts, solve kinds of problems now reserved for humans, and improve themselves. We think that a significant advance can be made in one or more of these problems if a carefully selected group of scientists work on it together for a summer.”
   - J. McCarthy (Dartmouth College), M. L. Minsky (Harvard), N. Rochester (I.B.M.), C. E. Shannon (Bell Telephone Laboratories), “A Proposal for the Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence,” 31 August 1955. Text on the Formal Reasoning Group mirror at Stanford.
   - https://www-formal.stanford.edu/jmc/history/dartmouth/dartmouth.html

2. The same proposal lists seven problems, including “How Can a Computer be Programmed to Use a Language,” “Neuron Nets,” “Self-Improvement,” and “Randomness and Creativity.” On creativity: “the difference between creative thinking and unimaginative competent thinking lies in the injection of a some randomness. The randomness must be guided by intuition to be efficient.”
   - Same proposal.
   - https://www-formal.stanford.edu/jmc/history/dartmouth/dartmouth.html

3. McCarthy’s own note on the copy he posted: “Text of the original 1955 Dartmouth Proposal.” “If you are interested in the exact typography, you will have to consult a paper copy.”
   - John McCarthy, Stanford, “A Proposal for the Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence.”
   - http://jmc.stanford.edu/articles/dartmouth.html

4. Joseph Weizenbaum, “ELIZA—A Computer Program for the Study of Natural Language Communication Between Man and Machine,” *Communications of the ACM*, Volume 9, Number 1, January 1966, pp. 36–45: “ELIZA is a program operating within the MAC time-sharing system at MIT which makes certain kinds of natural language conversation between man and computer possible.” It ran in MAD-SLIP on the IBM 7094 (the Stanford scan’s OCR reads the model number badly in one place). “Its name was chosen to emphasize that it may be incrementally improved by its users … Like the Eliza of Pygmalion fame, it can be made to appear even more civilized, the relation of appearance to reality, however, remaining in the domain of the playwright.”
   - Scan hosted for Stanford course CS124 of the January 1966 *CACM* paper.
   - https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs124/p36-weizenabaum.pdf

5. Weizenbaum’s own warning in that paper: once a program is explained, “its magic crumbles away” and “the observer says to himself ‘I could have written that’. With that thought he moves the program in question from the shelf marked ‘intelligent’, to that reserved for curios.”
   - Same *CACM* paper.
   - https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs124/p36-weizenabaum.pdf

6. A 2026 public fact, from the European Commission’s own implementation guidance, file path dated July 2026: “The AI Act applies from 2 August 2026, subject to the staggered dates of application set out in Article 113.” Prohibited practices had already applied since 2 February 2025. Some product-safety high-risk rules “apply from 2 August 2027.” High-risk systems placed on the market before 2 August 2026 are covered “only if, as from that date, those systems are subject to significant changes in their designs.”
   - European Commission, Futurium, “Implementation Guidance for the EU AI Act,” PDF dated in the URL July 2026. Opened 9 October 2026.
   - https://futurium.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2026-07/Implementation-Guidance-EU-AI-Act_1.pdf

## 5. Steven Spielberg

**Odd detail.** He named the mechanical shark after his lawyer. In *Empire*, 28 September 2014: “I named the shark Bruce after my lawyer, Bruce Ramer. I would never have named the shark after my attorney if my attorney didn’t have a great sense of humour.” He also said, “I had a love-hate relationship with that mechanical shark. On the one hand, he caused unprecedented delays in the shooting. On the other hand, he gave us all careers.”

**Shaky.** “Rejected by USC three times” appears as a clause in a 2019 *Hollywood Reporter* profile of the USC dean, not as a quotation from Spielberg. What he himself is quoted as saying, in the *Irish Examiner* of 3 October 2004, is one admissions encounter: an officer with a C-average rule told him, “If you can make a basket or throw a football, you can get in here, too.” The story that he sneaked onto the Universal lot in a suit was not on any page opened here. Do not use it. The lot story that is sourced is the opposite of a break-in: the *Orca* sat on the Universal lot and was later chopped up.

1. On the shark breaking, in his own words: the mechanical mouth could not open and close under a tight polyurethane skin, so the model has “an overhanging upper mandible. A real shark doesn’t have this.” “It was the only way in 1974 we could get the mouth to open and close.” Bob Mattey “was not able to solve it.” The delays, he says, forced the film to hide the shark, which “is what really made Jaws an exercise in suspense.”
   - Steven Spielberg, interviewed by Ian Freer, “Inside Steven Spielberg’s Jaws Vault,” *Empire*, 28 September 2014.
   - https://www.empireonline.com/movies/features/steven-spielberg-jaws-vault/

2. The Universal lot, from the same interview: “The Orca used to be sitting right here on the Universal lot. It was out here for at least the first 20 years after the film was released in ’75.” He would sit in it and remember “the fear of coming to work every day and being afraid that this week could be my last week as the director of Jaws.” Then “because of the amount of mildew, and rot and infestation of termites, someone had taken it upon themselves to literally chop the Orca into a million pieces.” He called Sid Sheinberg. What he kept: “the steering wheel on the flying bridge, one of the propellors and the anchor.”
   - Same *Empire* interview, 28 September 2014.
   - https://www.empireonline.com/movies/features/steven-spielberg-jaws-vault/

3. There were two Orcas: “a main picture boat and an Orca that was built to sink then resurface in a little under 45 minutes for take two.” Editing was so slow that “if Verna [Fields] had the chance to make three splices in one day, that was a red-letter day. I was averaging about three shots a day.”
   - Same *Empire* interview.
   - https://www.empireonline.com/movies/features/steven-spielberg-jaws-vault/

4. About three months before shooting he tried to quit *Jaws* for another film. Producers Dick Zanuck and David Brown “showed up in their crew T-shirts, and the second I saw them in their T-shirts, I started laughing and they started laughing and I said, ‘Never mind.’”
   - Same *Empire* interview.
   - https://www.empireonline.com/movies/features/steven-spielberg-jaws-vault/

5. USC: “Spielberg recalls: ‘I said: Well, I understand you allow people in football and basketball to play here and they have C averages. He said: If you can make a basket or throw a football, you can get in here, too.’” The paper says he “was rejected by an admissions officer who deemed his grade C average unacceptable” and “went on to study at Long Beach University, before dropping out.”
   - *Irish Examiner*, “Spielberg recalls university rejection,” 3 October 2004.
   - https://www.irishexaminer.com/lifestyle/arid-30169309.html

6. The three-rejection claim, stated but not quoted from him: USC dean Elizabeth Daley’s school benefited from “non-alumni like Steven Spielberg (who has donated millions even though he got rejected from the school three times and instead went to Cal State Long Beach).”
   - Stephen Galloway, *The Hollywood Reporter*, 1 March 2019.
   - https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/lifestyle/lifestyle-news/how-usc-film-school-got-millions-steven-spielberg-1190637/

An NPR feature on the three sharks (“Hunting Bruce,” 2 June 2010) was found and not opened; the fetch timed out. The Bruce-the-lawyer fact above is Spielberg’s, in *Empire*.

## 7. Angelina Jolie

**Odd detail.** The “vial of blood” necklace, as Billy Bob Thornton described it to *Rolling Stone* on 17 November 2025: “People still talk about the vials of blood. Which never existed. We each had a little locket, literally with a drop of blood in them. That’s a romantic little idea, and that’s all that was. But by the time it’s over, we’re vampires.”
