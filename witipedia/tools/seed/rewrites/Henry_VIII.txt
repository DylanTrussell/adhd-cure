'''Henry VIII''' was King of England from 1509 until his death in 1547, married six times, and wrote a hit song. The song, "Pastime with Good Company", survives in manuscript and is attributed to him.<ref name="starkey">David Starkey, ''Six Wives: The Queens of Henry VIII'' (2003).</ref>

== The wives ==
The old schoolroom rhyme gives the outcomes in order: divorced, beheaded, died, divorced, beheaded, survived. Catherine of Aragon's marriage was annulled; Anne Boleyn was beheaded in 1536; Jane Seymour died after childbirth in 1537; Anne of Cleves had the marriage annulled in 1540 and received a generous settlement and a house; Catherine Howard was beheaded in 1542; Catherine Parr outlived him.<ref name="starkey" />

== Defender of the Faith ==
In 1521 Pope Leo X gave Henry the title "Fidei Defensor" for a book Henry had written against Martin Luther. After the break with Rome in the 1530s, Henry kept the title, and Parliament confirmed it in 1544. British coins still carry the abbreviation "F.D."<ref name="scar">J. J. Scarisbrick, ''Henry VIII'' (1968).</ref>

== The Groom of the Stool ==
Henry employed a courtier, the Groom of the Stool, whose duties included attending him on his close-stool toilet. The post was one of the most sought-after at court because it gave a man private access to the king every day.<ref name="starkey" />

== Size ==
The Royal Armouries' collection includes his armour from different years. His armour from his early twenties has a waist of roughly 32 to 35 inches, depending on the source. By about 1540 the waist of his armour measures about 54 inches. He also had a long-running ulcerated leg.<ref name="ra">Royal Armouries, notes on the armours of Henry VIII.</ref>

== The jousting accident ==
In January 1536 he fell in a joust and was unconscious for about two hours. Historians argue it altered his health and temperament for the worse.<ref name="scar" />

== The coffin ==
The tale that his coffin burst open in 1547 on the way to Windsor and that dogs licked the spilled remains is told in later Protestant accounts, usually traced to a sermon by the friar William Peto and repeated by chroniclers such as John Foxe. It was told after the fact as a story about divine judgment, and is not a documented event.<ref name="foxe">John Foxe, ''Actes and Monuments'' (1563 and later editions).</ref>
