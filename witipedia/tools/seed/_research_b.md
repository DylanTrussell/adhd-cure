The British Museum object page for EA41748 (https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA41748) stopped at a bot check and did not return a catalogue record.

### Facts

1. Britannica: “The name of the letter in the Phoenician period resembled the Hebrew name aleph meaning ‘ox’; the form is thought to derive from an earlier symbol resembling the head of an ox. The letter was taken over by the Greeks in the form of alpha.” In Phoenician it “stood for a species of breathing, as vowels were not represented in the Semitic alphabets.” The article was last revised by Michael Ray; no calendar date is printed on the page. https://www.britannica.com/topic/A-letter

2. Britannica Kids dates the picture-sign: an ox-head “in Egyptian hieroglyphic writing” and “in a very early Semitic writing used in about 1500 bc on the Sinai Peninsula.” “In about 1000 bc, in Byblos and other Phoenician and Canaanite centers, the sign was given a linear form.” In Semitic languages “this sign was called aleph, meaning ‘ox.’” The Greeks, having “no use for the aleph sound, the glottal stop,” used it for the vowel *a* and renamed it alpha. “The Romans took this sign over into Latin.” https://kids.britannica.com/students/article/A-a/272668

3. Same kids’ page: the English small *a* “first took shape in Greek handwriting.” “In about the 4th century ad this was given a circular shape with a projection. This shape was the parent of both the English handwritten character and the printed small a.” https://kids.britannica.com/students/article/A-a/272668

4. British Museum exhibition guide, label for the sandstone sphinx: “The base of the sphinx displays early alphabetic signs beneath the Egyptian hieroglyphs. This script called Proto-Sinaitic is an ancestor of the Latin alphabet. It was probably used by local people working as guides or traders in the Sinai desert. About 1800 BC, Serabit el-Khadim, sandstone British Museum, EA41748.” PDF dated October 2022 in the file path. https://www.britishmuseum.org/sites/default/files/2022-10/Hieroglyphs_unlocking_ancient_Egypt_large_print_guide_The_British_Museum_2.pdf

5. *Current World Archaeology*, 24 May 2019: the figure is “carved out of red sandstone around 1800 BC,” length “23.7cm,” found 1904–1905 by Flinders Petrie’s Egypt Exploration Fund team at Serabit el-Khadim. “An ox-head (the second character from the left on the base) is a forerunner of the Latin alphabet’s letter A.” Hieroglyphs on the right shoulder read “beloved of Hathor, mistress of turquoise.” Alan Gardiner transcribed one inscription as “mahbalt” (“beloved of the Lady”). The sphinx was then “on loan from the British Museum.” https://www.world-archaeology.com/world/africa/egypt/object-lesson-serabit-sphinx/

6. Oxford Dictionaries, quoting Samuel Morse’s count of printers’ type: E 12,000; T 9,000; “A, I, N, O, S” together at 8,000 each. The same page’s own count of letters in main entries of the *Concise Oxford Dictionary* (11th edition, revised 2004) puts A at 8.4966%, second only to E at 11.1607%. Taking Q as 1, A is listed at 43.31. Archived 22 April 2015. https://web.archive.org/web/20150422065653/http://www.oxforddictionaries.com/words/what-is-the-frequency-of-the-letters-of-the-alphabet-in-english

7. Peter Norvig, using Google Books English 1-grams (version 20120701), words of A–Z only with at least 100,000 mentions: “There were 3,563,505,777,820 letters mentioned.” A is third: “A 286.5 B 8.04%,” behind E “445.2 B 12.49%” and T “330.5 B 9.28%.” The order he prints is ETAOIN SRHLDCU, against the typesetters’ ETAOIN SHRDLU. The page is framed as a reply to a letter from Mark Mayzner dated 17 December 2012. http://norvig.com/mayzner.html

8. Mount Holyoke’s library, 30 March 2023: “In 1897, MHC developed the letter grading system, becoming the first recorded school to do so.” The same note says Yale in the 1780s used Latin grades and William & Mary used numbers. https://lits.mtholyoke.edu/news/2023-03-30/know-our-history-grading-mhc

9. A peer-reviewed teaching paper gives a different chronology for the same college: “in 1884, Mount Holyoke was grading on a system including ‘A,’ ‘B,’ ‘C,’ ‘D,’ and ‘E.’ Each letter corresponded to a range of percentage scores, with lower than 75% equating to an ‘E’ and indicating failure. Mount Holyoke added an ‘F’ grade (for failing) to the scale in 1898.” It also records a Harvard “B” in 1883, and Yale in 1785 sorting seniors into Optimi, second Optimi, Inferiores, and Pejores. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4041495/

### True but funny

Samuel Morse, counting metal type so he could give the shortest dots to the commonest letters, parked A in a five-way tie at 8,000 with I, N, O and S. E, the letter that actually runs English, had 12,000. Oxford Dictionaries, archived 22 April 2015: https://web.archive.org/web/20150422065653/http://www.oxforddictionaries.com/words/what-is-the-frequency-of-the-letters-of-the-alphabet-in-english

### Flag

Do not treat “1897, first letter grades” and “1884, A–E at Mount Holyoke” as the same fact. The college’s own 2023 page and the journal article were both opened; they do not agree. The British Museum label calls the script an ancestor of the Latin alphabet. It does not, on the page opened, identify the ox-head. That identification is *Current World Archaeology*’s.

## 8. Henry VIII

### Pages opened

### Facts

1. Royal Armouries: in 1520 “He was 29 years old, 188 centimetres tall (six foot, two inches).” The foot-combat armour for the Field of the Cloth of Gold “weighs 42.6 kilograms (approximately 96 pounds), which is twice the weight of a normal battle armour.” Object cited as II.6, on display in the Tournament Gallery, Leeds. “The large cod-piece is also a fashion statement, not a boast.” https://royalarmouries.org/objects-and-stories/stories/henry-viiis-foot-combat-armour

2. Same page: “The right neckguard has not been fitted and the armour was described as still being ‘black from the hammer’ almost a century on.” Reason: “Only three months before the tournament, Francis decided that the foot combats were instead going to be fought over the barrier.” Henry already had the fully enclosing armour. A second, tonlet armour (II.7) was “hastily assembled.” The tournament proper “began on Monday 11 June and lasted for eleven days.” “One French knight died tilting against his brother.” https://royalarmouries.org/objects-and-stories/stories/henry-viiis-foot-combat-armour

3. *Guardian*, 31 March 2009, quoting measurements at the Tower exhibition and “the Royal Armouries academic director, Graeme Rimer”: at 23, “6ft 1in,” “34.7in waist,” “41.7in chest.” Field of the Cloth of Gold armour: “36.1in waist and 41.8in chest.” Foot-combat armour that year: “37.9in waist and 44in chest.” “Twenty years later his armour for a tournament to celebrate May Day has him ballooned up to a 51in waist and 54.5in chest.” Rimer: “Armour has to fit quite closely.” https://www.theguardian.com/artanddesign/2009/apr/01/heritage-monarchy-henry-viii

4. Historic Royal Palaces names the six “in order, Katherine of Aragon, Anne Boleyn, Jane Seymour, Anne of Cleves, Catherine Howard and Katherine Parr,” and quotes the rhyme “divorced, beheaded, died; divorced, beheaded, survived.” https://www.hrp.org.uk/hampton-court-palace/history-and-stories/henry-viii-s-wives-six-queens-six-women/

5. Same HRP page, outcomes it actually states: Katherine “successfully argued her case in court” at Blackfriars (“Alas, Sir, where have I offended you?…”). 6. DIAMM on British Library Add. MS 31922, “Henry VIII Manuscript,” about 1510–20: the census of the book lists “Henry VIII [The Kynge h. viii]-34,” meaning 34 pieces ascribed to the king in that source, inside a book of 108 or 109 items (the page uses both counts). Bought by the British Museum from the Lamb family through Quaritch in 1882. https://www.diamm.ac.uk/sources/1238/

7. Pittaway, part 1: “People have claimed that Henry VIII (1491–1547) wrote it for Anne Boleyn, with no evidence that he wrote it or even that the song existed in his lifetime… Henry VIII died in 1547. Greensleeves first appeared in 1580.” He traces one bad inference to William Chappell, *Popular Music of the Olden Time* (1859), who moved from a 1598 remark about *The Lord of Lorne* to “Green Sleeves must be a tune of Henry’s reign.” https://earlymusicmuse.com/greensleeves1of3mythology/

8. Pittaway, part 2: “on 3rd September, Richard Jones was licensed to print A New Northern Dittye of ye Lady Greene Sleeves.” The surviving words are in Richard Jones’s *A Handful of Pleasant Delights* (1584), “A new Courtly Sonet of the Lady Green sleeues. To the new tune of Greensleeues.” The chord plan is a passamezzo antico verse plus a romanesca chorus, an Italian ground Pittaway says was only becoming normal in English lute writing after John Johnson’s 1577 court appointment. https://earlymusicmuse.com/greensleeves2of3history/

### True but funny

The armour Royal Armouries calls an engineering one-off was never finished, because Francis I changed the rules three months out. The right neck-guard was never fitted, and nearly a hundred years later the suit was still “black from the hammer.” The codpiece, the museum adds, “is a fashion statement, not a boast.” https://royalarmouries.org/objects-and-stories/stories/henry-viiis-foot-combat-armour

### Flag

Height: Royal Armouries says 6 ft 2 in at age 29 in 1520. The Guardian says 6 ft 1 in at age 23. Weight of the foot armour: the museum says 42.6 kg and about 96 lb; the Guardian says “94lb or 42.7kg” for armour “made for 28-year-old Henry.” The 51-inch waist is the Guardian quoting the Armouries’ academic director, not a sentence on the Armouries page that was opened. Jane “begging him not to destroy the monasteries” is marked on the HRP page as allegation (“one story suggests,” “allegedly”). Greensleeves is debunked in a specialist essay, not in a British Library catalogue label. DIAMM does not, on the page opened, name “Pastime with Good Company”; what it does say is that 34 pieces in Add. MS 31922 are entered as “The Kynge h. viii.”

The Guardian also quotes keeper of armour Thom Richardson saying NASA studied the Field of the Cloth of Gold armour when designing the first spacesuits. No NASA page was opened, so that stays a quotation of the keeper, not a confirmed NASA study.

## 10. Joseph Stalin

No joke in this section is about the dead. The famine, the executions of statisticians, and the Doctors’ Plot are stated as the sources state them.

### Pages opened

1. Simon Sebag Montefiore, “Before the terror,” *The Guardian*, 18 May 2007 — https://www.theguardian.com/books/2007/may/19/featuresreviews.guardianreview32
2. Catherine Merridale, “The 1937 census and the limits of Stalinist rule,” *The Historical Journal* 39, no. 1 (March 1996), pp. 225–240, Cambridge Core, published online 11 February 2009 — https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/historical-journal/article/abs/1937-census-and-the-limits-of-stalinist-rule/EA2018DAD588327DE868929E99DE206F

4. Jackie Mansky, “The True Story of the Death of Stalin,” *Smithsonian Magazine*, 10 October 2017 — https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/true-story-death-stalin-180965119/

### Facts

1. Montefiore, *Guardian*, 18 May 2007: in 1895, aged 17, at the Tiflis seminary, he showed poems to Prince Ilya Chavchavadze, who “chose five to publish in Iveria” under the nickname “Soselo.” Chavchavadze called him that “young man with the burning eyes.” “Soselo was admired as a poet before anyone had ever heard of ‘Stalin’, the name he did not coin until 1912.” https://www.theguardian.com/books/2007/may/19/featuresreviews.guardianreview32

2. Same essay: the children’s anthology *Deda Ena* “included Stalin’s first published poem, ‘Morning,’ in its 1916 edition, where it remained (sometimes ascribed to Stalin, sometimes not) up to the days of Brezhnev.” “They were usually published as ‘Anonymous.’” Other titles he discusses: “To the Moon” and “Old Ninika,” the latter in the weekly *Kvali*. https://www.theguardian.com/books/2007/may/19/featuresreviews.guardianreview32

3. Same essay: “Stalin never publicly acknowledged his own poems.” For the official 70th birthday in 1949, Lavrenti Beria “secretly commissioned” translators, “including Pasternak and Andrei Tarkovsky,” to make a Russian edition of the five. “They were not told who the author was.” One of them “thought ‘this work is worthy of the Stalin Prize first rank.’” Then “they received the stern order, clearly from Stalin himself, to stop work.” https://www.theguardian.com/books/2007/may/19/featuresreviews.guardianreview32

4. Merridale, *Historical Journal*, 1996: “In January 1937 the government of the USSR conducted its first population census in eleven years.” Questions included, “for the first and last time, a question about the respondent’s religion.” “The information gathered was exceptionally thorough and complete. Crucially, however, the census was entirely suppressed, and the officials responsible for organizing it promptly arrested and executed.” https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/historical-journal/article/abs/1937-census-and-the-limits-of-stalinist-rule/EA2018DAD588327DE868929E99DE206F

5. Merridale’s abstract ties the suppression “largely” to “the sensitivity of evidence of high mortality during the famine of 1932–3.” In the notes printed with the article she records that Andreev, Darskii and Kharkova “suggest roughly 8 million excess deaths in 1932–3,” and she also prints the older, wider estimates (Maksudov 9.8 million plus or minus 3 million; Lorimer 4–5 million). Those are citations on her page, not one agreed total. https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/historical-journal/article/abs/1937-census-and-the-limits-of-stalinist-rule/EA2018DAD588327DE868929E99DE206F

6. Fitzpatrick, *Guardian*, 18 August 2016, on Rubenstein: after a stroke “on the night of 1 March 1953,” security “could [not] prevent him lying for hours in his own urine, paralysed, and without the ability to scream.” “Servants and guards were worried about his non-appearance but afraid to disturb him. When his colleagues were finally called, they hovered uncertainly before finally calling a doctor – not Stalin’s personal physician, since he and a number of his colleagues had recently been arrested as traitors in the Doctors’ Plot.” https://www.theguardian.com/books/2016/aug/18/last-days-of-stalin-joshua-rubinstein-review

7. Same review, the treatment Rubenstein records: “absolute quiet; the application of eight medical leeches to his ears; a cold compress on his head; an enema of milk of magnesia; and the removal of his false teeth.” “Stalin died without regaining consciousness on the evening of 5 March.” “His closest colleagues did not kill him, despite much speculation that Joshua Rubenstein rightly deflates.” Within weeks of the death, “the (mainly Jewish) Kremlin physicians accused in the Doctors’ Plot” were “exonerated and freed.” https://www.theguardian.com/books/2016/aug/18/last-days-of-stalin-joshua-rubinstein-review

8. *Smithsonian*, 10 October 2017: at dinner the night of 28 February 1953, Stalin asked “whether confessions had been extracted for a trial he would soon oversee.” “That winter, Stalin had been waging a witch hunt against Kremlin physicians, many of whom were Jewish, claiming they murdered top Soviet officials in a ‘doctors’ plot.’ The trial against the Kremlin doctors was to commence within weeks.” Beria, reached by phone, “demanded they tell no one of Stalin’s illness.” Doctors were called “around 7 in the morning.” They found blood pressure “190/110.” “His death was recorded at 9:50 p.m.” on 5 March. https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/true-story-death-stalin-180965119/

### True but funny

For his 70th birthday, Beria hired Pasternak and other translators to put the teenage “Soselo” poems into Russian, and did not tell them who had written the poems. One reader thought the anonymous verses deserved the Stalin Prize, first class. Stalin then ordered the project stopped. He did not want the edition. Montefiore, *Guardian*, 18 May 2007: https://www.theguardian.com/books/2007/may/19/featuresreviews.guardianreview32

### Flag

Poisoning is not a fact here. *Smithsonian* says a warfarin theory, discussed by Jonathan Brent and Vladimir Naumov, “will likely forever remain a matter of speculation.” Fitzpatrick writes that Rubenstein “rightly deflates” the idea that the inner circle killed him. *Smithsonian* also notes that Zhores and Roy Medvedev doubt the story that the staff were simply too frightened to open the door. The “afraid to disturb him” line is Fitzpatrick’s summary of Rubenstein, not a document quoted in full. A population total of 162 million for the 1937 census was not on any non-Wikipedia page opened, so it is left out. Montefiore’s account of the 1907 Tiflis robbery is in the same essay and is not used as a light detail.
